A decision frame for marketing investment

By Shashikant · Updated 19 November 2026 · How this hub is made and checked

⚡ In one minute

Six questions to ask before approving any marketing spend, from what the money is for to whether the business is ready for the attention.

Most organisations and individuals spend money on marketing without a clear, independent answer to three questions: what the money is supposed to achieve, how they will know if it achieved it, and whose interests are shaping the recommendation. This produces activity, reports and expenditure while clarity remains weak.

The pattern is structural. Incentives often favour volume. Available metrics favour what platforms can optimise. Buyers rarely develop independent judgement at the same speed as the systems they are sold. The result is repeated spending under conditions of false confidence.

This page exists to make that failure mode visible and to offer a stable set of questions that remain useful across channels, platforms and years.

The six governing questions

  1. What is the actual job of this money?
    Awareness, response from people who already know the brand, or both? If the job is unclear, every later metric becomes negotiable.
  2. What evidence would show that job was done?
    Define the evidence before the spend, not after. Platform conversions are only one possible form of evidence, and often an incomplete one.
  3. How much of the expected result is likely to be incremental?
    How much would have happened anyway? Brand search, existing demand and remarketing frequently inflate reported results.
  4. Who benefits if more is spent here?
    Platforms, media owners and many intermediaries earn more when more is spent. That incentive is not automatically aligned with the buyer’s interest.
  5. What will we stop doing if the evidence is weak?
    Without a pre-agreed stop or change rule, spending continues by inertia and sunk-cost pressure.
  6. Is the rest of the business ready for the attention this may create?
    Marketing multiplies whatever the business already is. Weak offer, slow Japanese-language support, unclear fulfilment or poor experience turns attention into damage.

Why these questions matter more than channel knowledge

Channel guides, cost tables and platform recommendations are useful only after a decision frame exists. Without the frame, more information usually increases activity rather than clarity. With the frame, the same information becomes evidence that can be accepted or rejected.

The most expensive mistakes rarely come from choosing the wrong single channel. They come from entering significant spend without stable answers to the questions above.

Readiness. Japanese-language support, a clear return policy and reliable delivery are not yet in place. The result: the brand fixes these first and starts with a ¥500,000 test rather than ¥2,000,000. The numbers are invented to show the reasoning, and real decisions need real margins and data.

Who benefits. The agency fee is 20% of spend, so more spend means more fee. The brand sets a monthly cap and asks for efficiency reports. The stop rule. The average first order is ¥6,000 with a 50% gross margin, so each first order earns ¥3,000 before ad costs. If the cost of a new customer stays above ¥3,000 after six weeks, spending pauses unless repeat-purchase data justifies it.

The job. The brand is unknown in Japan, so the job is awareness plus first purchases, not harvesting existing demand. The evidence. It decides to count new customers and brand-name searches, not only platform-reported sales. Incrementality. Retargeting and brand-search ads will claim sales that might have happened anyway, so those are reported separately.

Illustrative, not a real company A foreign skincare brand plans ¥2,000,000 of advertising in Japan next month. Walking through the six questions changes the plan.

A worked example

How to use this frame

Before approving or recommending significant marketing spend, walk through the six questions in order. If several answers are vague, the rational next step is usually not more media. It is clarifying the job, the evidence standard, the readiness of the business, or the incentive structure of the advice being received.

The rest of this hub — cost ranges, media explanations, measurement notes and scenarios — can be read as supporting material once these questions are active.
