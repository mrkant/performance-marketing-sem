How advertising results are measured in Japan: ROI, ROAS, CPA and what cannot be measured

By Shashikant · Updated 19 November 2026 · How this hub is made and checked

⚡ In one minute

A ROAS of 3.0 can still mean zero profit. Learn ROI, CPA and CAC, what each medium can and cannot measure, and how attribution splits credit.

Every advertising quote eventually leads to one question: did it work? Answering it means knowing which numbers exist, how they are calculated, what they leave out, and how they connect to what you pay. This page explains the main measures in plain language and is useful whichever medium you use.

The core measures

Measure Formula It tells you Watch out for
ROAS Revenue from ads ÷ ad spend Revenue per yen of advertising Ignores product cost, fees and returns
CPA Ad spend ÷ number of conversions Cost of one sale or enquiry Depends on how a conversion is defined
CAC All acquisition costs ÷ new customers True cost of winning a customer Often leaves out salaries, tools and agency fees
ROI (Profit − cost) ÷ cost Return after costs Needs an agreed profit figure
LTV Average gross profit per customer over time What a customer is worth Based on estimates for new customers
Blended efficiency Total revenue ÷ total marketing spend Whole-business view across media Does not show which channel worked

Clicks, impressions and views are activity measures. They show that something ran, not that it paid off.

Why ROAS alone misleads

Illustrative, not a real company Suppose a shop spends ¥1,000,000 on ads and the ad platform reports ¥3,000,000 in sales. ROAS is 3.0, which sounds healthy. If the products carry a 40% gross margin, gross profit is ¥1,200,000. After the ¥1,000,000 ad spend and a ¥200,000 agency fee, nothing is left. The campaign broke even, and returns, shipping or discounts could turn it into a loss. A good agency report shows profit after costs, not only ROAS.

How each medium is usually measured

Medium Usually measured Usually not measured well
Search and social ads Clicks, conversions, CPA, ROAS in the platform and in analytics Cross-device journeys, effect on later brand search, views without clicks
SEO Rankings, organic visits, conversions Value of pages that help a sale without being the last visit
Influencers Links, promotion codes, saves, comments Influence on people who buy later through search
Train and station ads QR scans, unique web address, promotion codes, brand-search changes Who saw it and how often; store visits unless tracked
Television Audience ratings from research firms, search and sales changes during the run Individual response
Newspapers, magazines, radio Reply codes, phone numbers, web addresses used only in that ad Readership and listening actually reaching your buyers
LINE accounts Friends, blocks, opens, clicks, redemptions Sales that happen later in a store

Attribution: who gets the credit

Attribution is the rule that decides which ad gets credit for a sale. Last-click gives all credit to the final click, which favours search ads that capture people already convinced. Platform reports often claim credit for the same sale, so adding them up can exceed real sales. Data-driven models spread credit using patterns in the data. Incrementality tests go further: they hold back advertising in one group or area and compare sales with an exposed group, which shows what the ads added. Tests need time and volume, so they suit larger budgets.

What "not measured" really means

Unmeasured does not mean ineffective. Brand and mass media often work through effects that are hard to trace: a poster raises recognition, and the sale later appears as a search or a store visit. The honest approach is to say which effects are measured, which are estimated and which are assumed. Be careful with reports that present estimates as facts, or that show only metrics that look good.

How measurement connects to what you pay

A fee based on a percentage of ad spend rewards spending more, so ask how efficiency will be improved. A performance fee needs a written definition of a result: a purchase, a qualified lead, or a form submission, and whether cancellations and returns are deducted. Fixed retainers need agreed deliverables and reporting. In every case, make sure you own or can access the ad accounts, analytics and customer data, and that the reporting period and format are written into the contract.

Questions to ask before you pay

What exactly counts as a result? Which tool is the source of truth? Are numbers after refunds and returns? How do you handle sales that several ads claim? What will you do if the target is missed? Who can see the raw accounts? What is reported when something is not measurable?

These questions are protective. They do not require hostility toward any platform. They require recognising that the party recommending the tool and the party paying for the media do not always have identical interests.

  • How much of the expected volume is likely to come from brand and existing demand?
  • What controls exist for placements and queries?
  • How will we distinguish platform-reported success from incremental business results?
  • What is the exit path if performance is poor or opaque?

None of this means automated campaigns are never useful. It means they require unusually clear conversion definitions, careful brand exclusions where available, strong creative assets, and a willingness to examine results beyond the headline ROAS. Buyers should ask, before accepting a recommendation to move significant budget into such systems:

  • Limited transparency about where budget is actually spent and which queries or placements drive results.
  • Tendency to capture branded search and remarketing demand, which inflates reported efficiency.
  • Difficulty controlling or even seeing low-quality placements in some cases.
  • Strong commercial incentives for the platform and for many agencies to recommend higher automation and higher budgets.

Google Performance Max and similar fully automated campaign types are designed to optimise delivery across multiple surfaces using machine learning. They can reduce the operational burden of managing many separate campaigns. They also concentrate several of the risks described above.

Automated and black-box campaign types (including Performance Max)

The educational point is not that platform metrics are useless. It is that they are incomplete. Business decisions require an additional layer of judgement about what the numbers are actually capturing.

  • Brand cannibalisation — Automated systems often find that the cheapest conversions come from people already searching for the brand name. The campaign takes credit for demand that was largely already present.
  • Soft conversion definitions — If the system is optimising for form fills, page views or low-intent actions, it will efficiently find more of those actions, many of which have little downstream value.
  • Channel opacity — When budget can move freely across Search, Display, YouTube and other surfaces without clear controls, reported results become harder to interpret and harder to improve.
  • Learning-period volatility — Early performance can look strong or weak for reasons that do not persist. Acting too quickly on short windows is a common source of waste.

When platform numbers systematically mislead

  • Separate brand and non-brand performance wherever the platform allows it.
  • Treat strong reported ROAS on campaigns that include brand traffic with caution until you understand the mix.
  • For larger spends, consider simple geo or time-based tests, or independent incrementality measurement, rather than relying only on platform attribution.

Practical implications:

When a campaign captures a large share of branded search or existing demand, reported ROAS can look excellent while incremental return is modest. This is especially common with automated campaign types that are allowed to bid on brand terms and remarketing audiences. A healthy-looking dashboard is not proof that the spend created proportional new value.

Platform dashboards report conversions that occurred after someone saw or clicked an ad. They do not automatically tell you how many of those conversions would have happened anyway. The difference is incrementality — the extra business actually caused by the advertising.
