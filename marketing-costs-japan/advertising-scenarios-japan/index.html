Illustrative advertising scenarios in Japan: what to watch for

By Shashikant · Updated 19 November 2026 · How this hub is made and checked

⚡ In one minute

Invented stories showing how healthy-looking numbers, unmeasured posters and service gaps change results. None is a real company.

These scenarios are invented teaching examples, not case studies of real companies. They use round numbers to show how decisions, costs and measurement fit together. Real results depend on the business, the season and the execution.

Scenario 1: A shop with a healthy ROAS and no profit

Illustrative, not a real company An online shop spends ¥1,000,000 a month on search and social ads and sees a ROAS of 3.0. Its margin is 40% and the agency charges 20% of spend. After costs the campaign roughly breaks even, and the shop keeps paying for growth that adds no profit. What to learn: ask for profit after fees, returns and shipping. Raising the margin or the average order value often beats raising the budget.

Scenario 2: A poster campaign nobody can measure

Illustrative, not a real company A regional brand books a week of station posters for about ¥2,000,000. The poster has the brand name but no web address or code. Sales rise slightly that month, but a holiday also fell inside the period, so nobody can say why. What to learn: give every offline ad its own trace, such as a QR code, a promotion code or a dedicated address, and record brand searches before, during and after the run.

Scenario 3: A foreign cosmetics brand with a customer service gap

Illustrative, not a real company A brand enters Japan with strong creative and a localised website but no Japanese-language support and a vague return policy. Ads bring a steady stream of first orders, then reviews mention slow replies and unclear ingredient information. Conversion falls and the cost per customer rises. What to learn: advertising multiplies whatever the business already delivers, good or bad. Fix service, product information and claims compliance before scaling spend.

Scenario 4: Followers up, sales flat

Illustrative, not a real company A small restaurant pays ¥300,000 a month for social media management. Followers double in six months. Bookings are unchanged because posts carry no booking link and nobody tracks where reservations come from. What to learn: agree one business measure at the start, such as bookings, and build the path from the post to the booking so it can be counted.

Again: this is a teaching construct. Real budgets, reach and results depend on category, season, creative quality and execution. Use the structure — cost transparency, stakeholder lenses, funnel logic and the aware/unaware distinction — to pressure-test any large multi-channel proposal you receive.

The same spend feels wasteful when it is treated as a one-off burst with no tracking, no service readiness, and no link to a subsequent plan. In that case the organisation has mainly purchased expensive noise.

  • The business can actually serve the attention it buys.
  • There is a written learning plan and a measurement design that matches the media mix.
  • Creative, offer and experience are aligned.
  • Leadership has already decided what decision the results will inform.

If the brand is still unknown to most of the target, a pure performance dashboard will look disappointing even when the awareness job was done well. Agree in advance how much of the objective is “make more people know us” versus “convert people who already know us.”

  • Already-aware people: the campaign mainly refreshes salience and may accelerate a purchase that was already possible. Frequency and creative quality matter.
  • Newly reached people: the campaign creates the first memory. Clarity of the offer, trust signals and an easy next step matter even more.

A large multi-channel month reaches both people who already know the brand and people who do not. The value mix is different:

  • Clear primary KPI for the month and secondary diagnostics.
  • Creative and offer consistency across train screens, television, social and landing pages.
  • Tracking design: unique codes, URLs, QR codes, and a simple pre/during/post measurement plan for brand metrics.
  • Handover quality: what happens after the click or the QR scan?
  • Learning agenda: which two or three hypotheses will this month actually test?

Marketing lead

  • Total cash outlay and its timing (media is often paid in advance or on strict deadlines).
  • Fully loaded cost: media + production + agency + internal time + expected returns/refunds.
  • Which portion of the spend has a reasonably attributable near-term revenue effect, and which portion is brand investment that must be judged on longer or softer metrics?
  • Downside case: if digital conversion is significantly worse than the base case, what does the month still cost and what residual brand asset remains?
  • Opportunity cost: what else could this capital do in the same period?

Finance / CFO lens

  • Is the month coherent with a larger strategy (launch, seasonal peak, competitive response), or is it activity for its own sake?
  • Can the product, service capacity, Japanese-language support and fulfilment absorb a spike in attention without damaging ratings or trust?
  • What will we know at the end of the month that we did not know before — about message, audience or channel?
  • What is the decision this spend is meant to enable?

CEO / general manager

The offline media will almost never show a clean last-click ROAS. Their value appears as higher branded search, easier conversion rates on digital, and longer-term preference. Forcing every yen into a single ROAS number systematically undervalues the awareness layer.

  • People reached offline (train + TV): roughly 2–4 million (geography-dependent).
  • Of those, a fraction become newly aware or more familiar with the brand. Brand-lift studies for campaigns of this weight often show single-digit to low double-digit percentage point lifts among those exposed — the exact number is empirical, not assumed.
  • Digital channels: 8–20 million impressions → 150,000–400,000 clicks (highly creative- and bid-dependent) → 3,000–12,000 site actions or leads, depending on offer and page quality.
  • If the business converts 5–15% of qualified actions into customers, the month might produce a few hundred to a couple of thousand new customers from the paid digital layer alone, plus an unmeasured contribution from people who saw the offline media and later searched or bought.

Combined, a well-executed month at this scale can put the brand in front of a meaningful portion of the target demographic in the chosen geography, with offline media doing the heavy lifting on frequency and digital doing the heavy lifting on response.

  • Train / station (Tokyo focus) : repeated exposure to a large share of regular Yamanote and major-station commuters. Think hundreds of thousands to low millions of unique people seeing the message multiple times in a month, with high frequency among daily riders.
  • Regional / limited television : broad reach inside the chosen area, lower frequency unless the weight is heavy.
  • Paid digital : can be scaled to several million impressions and hundreds of thousands of clicks, but the audience overlaps only partially with the offline reach.
  • Influencers : deeper engagement with smaller, more targeted groups.

4. What each leader should look at

CEO / general manager : Is the month coherent with strategy? Can the business absorb the attention without damaging trust? What decision will the results inform?

Finance : Fully loaded cost, cash timing, which portion is near-term attributable vs brand investment, downside case, opportunity cost.

Marketing : Primary KPI, creative consistency, tracking design (unique codes/URLs), post-click experience, and a clear learning agenda.

5. Brand-aware versus newly reached

Common threads

Each scenario fails or succeeds on four points: a clear business goal, a result that can be measured, a customer experience that matches the promise, and a contract that makes responsibilities clear. Use them as a checklist when you read any proposal.

All numbers are invented for teaching and do not describe any real client, brand or agency.

They are teaching tools. Change the numbers to match your category and constraints. The useful part is the decision logic: what is tested first, what is measured, what is deferred, and where brand and service readiness limit how much media spend is rational. If a scenario assumes a functioning website and Japanese-language support, and your reality does not yet have those, the media plan should wait until they do.
