A practical, independent reference for understanding advertising and marketing costs, decisions and systems in Japan.

This hub exists to help people and organisations think more clearly before they spend money or make commitments. It explains how the main media are sold, what published sources say they cost, how results are (and are not) measured, and how advertising connects to brand, service and customer experience.

It is written as an educational resource. It is not an agency, not a consultancy, and not an offer of services. Figures are published ranges and teaching examples, not quotes. Real prices depend on timing, volume, location and negotiation.

If these questions cannot be answered with reasonable clarity, the rational first step is usually not more media. It is closing the gaps in offer, experience, tracking or service capacity.

  1. What is the primary job of this money — new awareness, response from people who already know us, or both? Are we measuring the job we actually bought?
  2. Can the website, offer, Japanese-language support and fulfilment absorb a rise in attention without quality falling?
  3. Who benefits if we spend more in this channel? Does that party’s incentive align with our business outcome?
  4. What portion of the expected results is likely to be incremental (would not have happened otherwise), and what portion may be cannibalisation or organic demand taking credit?
  5. If the early numbers look weak, what will we stop or change? If the numbers look strong, how will we check they are not inflated by brand search or low-quality conversions?
  6. Is the creative and the landing experience consistent with the promise? Advertising that over-promises relative to the actual experience damages trust faster than it builds it.

Before you spend serious money — a readiness frame

Advertising multiplies whatever the business already is. Media that creates attention without a coherent offer, usable experience and adequate service capacity usually multiplies disappointment. The cost tables on this site are only useful when read together with measurement reality, creative quality and operational readiness.

This hub exists to reduce that disadvantage. It does not replace professional advice or current quotes. It tries to give readers a clearer mental model so they ask better questions before they commit significant budget.

  • Information asymmetry — Platforms, media owners and many intermediaries know more about how the systems actually work (and where waste occurs) than the buyer does.
  • Misaligned incentives — The parties recommending channels and tools often earn more when more money is spent, not necessarily when the money is spent more efficiently or more honestly.
  • False confidence from metrics — Dashboards can show healthy ROAS or CPA while the underlying business effect (incrementality, customer quality, contribution margin, long-term brand) is weaker than the numbers suggest.

The market in one paragraph

Japan's total advertising spend in 2025 was about ¥8.06 trillion according to Dentsu. Internet advertising took roughly ¥4.05 trillion, or 50.2%, for the first time a majority. The four traditional mass media together were about ¥2.30 trillion: television about ¥1.76 trillion, newspapers about ¥314 billion, radio about ¥115 billion and magazines about ¥114 billion. Television is still large, but it is flat. Digital is where the growth is.

The pages linked below therefore discuss not only published cost ranges, but also what to ask before you buy, how results are (and are not) measured, and how advertising connects to brand, service and user experience. Use the cost tables to judge scale; use the surrounding guidance to decide whether the spend is likely to be coherent with the rest of the business.

Measurement works the same way. Some media give clean cost-per-acquisition numbers; others mainly deliver reach and frequency whose value appears later in search behaviour, brand preference or easier conversion on other channels. Forcing every medium into the same last-click ROI formula systematically undervalues awareness and overvalues the last click. The practical approach is to measure what each medium can honestly show, and to judge the whole system by business outcomes: new customers, contribution margin, retention and brand health.

Every figure on this hub is only part of the picture. Media creates attention. Whether that attention becomes a customer — and whether the customer returns — depends on the creative promise, the landing experience, the product or service quality, and the support that follows. A cheap media buy that drives traffic into a weak experience is still expensive. A more costly placement that is backed by a coherent offer and a ready service path can be efficient.
