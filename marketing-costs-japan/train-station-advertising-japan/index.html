Train and station advertising in Japan (JR and others)

By Shashikant · Updated 19 November 2026 · How this hub is made and checked

⚡ In one minute

A JR East hanging poster is listed at about ¥2.09 million for 7 days, and a 15-second Yamanote video spot at ¥4.8 million for a week. Add a QR code or promotion code so you can measure it.

Transport advertising is one of Japan's most distinctive media. Millions of people commute by rail every day, so the same message reaches the same people repeatedly.

Formats

Inside trains: hanging posters (中づり), window-top posters (まど上), door-side posters, strap ads and in-car video screens. In stations: wall posters (駅ばり), pillar wraps, large boards, floor ads, banners and digital signage. Many are sold per line and per week, in fixed time slots.

What it costs

Published range One agency lists a JR East hanging poster at about ¥2.09 million for 7 days including tax. For video, a 15-second spot on Yamanote Line trains for one week is listed at ¥4.8 million , and ¥87 million for 26 weeks. Less crowded Tokyo-area lines such as the Keiyo, Yokohama and Nambu lines are listed at about ¥300,000–350,000 for the same 15 seconds and 7 days. A JR East station network package, played every six minutes, is listed at ¥2.2 million per week excluding tax. Taxi monitor ads are listed at roughly ¥2–6 million depending on the company and area.

How it works

You choose a line, media type and week, supply artwork to a fixed specification, and the railway or its sales partner posts it. Railways sell through their own advertising subsidiaries and through specialist transport-ad agencies, and many media have limited slots, so booking and artwork deadlines matter. Verified JR East Marketing & Communications (jeki), a wholly owned JR East subsidiary, acts as the group's house agency and manages JR East's transit media. Its own published awards rules say that in fiscal 2023 it sold and managed station and vehicle media for JR East, Tsukuba Express, Yurikamome, Rinkai Line, JR Freight and Shinano Railway. Its media guide is at jeki.co.jp/transit_ad . Other railways, including private lines and subways, run their own sales channels, so check each operator's advertising page.

Why advertisers choose it

Interpretation Transit ads suit brands that want repeated, local, daily exposure: retailers near stations, employers recruiting, entertainment launches and campaigns tied to a city. They are weaker for tracking direct sales unless paired with a code, a URL or a search campaign.

How booking works in practice

Transit space is sold as numbered slots by line, medium and week, so the first decision is where your audience travels rather than how big your budget is. A guide from one agency directory advises choosing the exact placement first and then checking the contract period, because periods differ by medium. Many advertisers book through a transport-advertising specialist or a general agency, which handles the media owner, artwork specifications and posting.

Three common mistakes

Published guidance Published guides on JR East advertising point to three recurring problems. First, a poster that blends in with the many other ads around it. Second, creative that is not designed with the media owner's review in mind, since artwork is checked before posting. Third, difficulty seeing the effect, because a poster does not click through. Plan a way to measure before you book: a dedicated web address, a QR code, a promotion code, or a before-and-after check of brand searches.

Questions to ask when you contact a seller

Which lines and stations are available in my target weeks? What are the size and file specifications, and the artwork deadline? What review does artwork go through, and how long does it take? What are the cancellation terms? How is posting confirmed, with photos or a report? Is the price shown with or without tax, and does it include production and posting fees?

Who this suits

Interpretation Rail media suits brands that want repeated daily exposure among commuters and students in a defined area: retailers, employers, education, entertainment and consumer launches. It is a poor fit if you need immediate trackable sales and cannot support the ad with search or social campaigns.

High-visibility placements raise expectations. Ensure the brand assets, website language and service capacity are ready for the attention. A strong train campaign that leads to a weak digital or service experience wastes the premium paid for the placement.

Brand and experience implications

Direct response tracking is limited. Practical methods include unique URLs or QR codes, promotion codes, geo-based tests, search-volume changes and, for larger spends, brand-lift research. Treat transport media primarily as a reach and frequency tool, and design the rest of the funnel to capture the interest it creates.

Measurement approaches

Inventory is limited and popular periods book early. Digital screens and classic posters have different production requirements and different proof-of-posting practices. Ask for clear specifications, deadlines, cancellation terms and how placement will be evidenced. Separate media cost, production cost and any agency fee in the quote.

Buying practicalities

Commuting patterns create repeated exposure to the same people in a relatively uncluttered environment. That frequency is valuable for brand building and for products that benefit from being top-of-mind at the moment of decision. It is less suited to complex offers that need time and interaction to explain.

Sources and evidence notes

Before you pay: what to ask and how to measure it

Ask which lines and weeks are free, the artwork deadline, review time and cancellation terms. Put a QR code, unique web address or promotion code on the poster, and note brand-search changes before and during the run. Without these, you cannot tell what the poster did.
