Product Intelligence

Understanding what is selling, why products succeed or fail, and how to discover new product opportunities with discipline.

Product as the centre of value creation

Brand can create desire and distribution can create access, but the product is what the customer ultimately receives. Product intelligence examines fit with market needs, sources of differentiation, quality and performance, lifecycle dynamics, assortment logic and the signals that indicate where new opportunities may exist.

Core product concepts

Product-market fit

The degree to which the product satisfies a strong market need for a defined customer group. Fit is not binary; it is a matter of degree and can change as markets and competitors evolve.

Differentiation

Meaningful differences that customers notice and value, whether functional, emotional, aesthetic, experiential or economic. Differentiation that customers do not value or cannot perceive does not support pricing power.

Functional and emotional benefits

Functional benefits solve practical problems. Emotional benefits address identity, feeling and social meaning. Strong products usually deliver both, with one dominant.

Lifecycle and renewal

Products move through introduction, growth, maturity and decline. The speed of the cycle varies by category. Planned renewal, extension or replacement is a core strategic responsibility.

The Product Opportunity Discovery Model

A structured way to investigate existing products and to surface potential new propositions. All outputs at the opportunity stage remain hypotheses until validated.

1. What products are selling? ▼

Examine category rankings, retailer bestseller lists, marketplace visibility, and (where available) volume or value data from industry sources. Distinguish popularity from profitability. Note which product types, price tiers and formats are gaining or losing share.

2. Which customer segments purchase them? ▼

Identify primary buyers by demographics, life stage, income, lifestyle and usage occasion. Look for concentration: is demand broad or dependent on a narrow group?

3. What problems do they solve? ▼

Map functional jobs and emotional jobs. Separate the problem the customer is trying to solve from the features the product offers.

4. What makes them different? ▼

List claimed and observable points of difference. Test which differences are noticed by customers and which are only visible to the manufacturer.

5. What are customers praising and complaining about? ▼

Mine reviews, returns reasons, service tickets and social commentary. Praise reveals what to protect; complaints reveal friction and unmet expectations.

6. What price points are accepted? ▼

Observe actual transaction prices, promotional depth, and the presence or absence of price resistance. Note good-better-best structures and gaps between tiers.

7. Are purchases repeated? ▼

For consumables and semi-durables, repeat rate is a critical health indicator. Low repeat after trial often signals a product or experience problem rather than a pure awareness problem.

8. Are there underserved customers or occasions? ▼

Look for segments that buy reluctantly, that improvise solutions, or that are ignored by current assortments. Underserved does not automatically mean commercially attractive; further filtering is required.

9. What could be improved or recombined? ▼

Generate improvement hypotheses and adjacent product ideas. Keep them labelled as hypotheses. Prioritise those that address clear complaints or gaps and that fit the organisation’s capabilities.
