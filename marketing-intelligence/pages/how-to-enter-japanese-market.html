Purpose: An educational resource for international companies considering Japan — whether exploring the market for the first time or preparing a more substantial presence. Not legal, tax or investment advice.

A practical guide to researching Japan, understanding customers, choosing an entry strategy, localizing your business and building a sustainable presence in one of the world’s major economies.

Expanding into another country can create significant opportunities. However, entering a new market also requires careful planning, research and an understanding of local customers. Japan has an established business environment, sophisticated industries, developed infrastructure and customers with a wide range of needs.

For an international company, Japan may offer opportunities in technology, software, manufacturing, professional services, consumer products, healthcare-related industries, tourism and many other sectors. However, the decision to enter Japan should not be based only on the size of its economy. A company needs to understand whether its offering solves a genuine problem for Japanese customers, whether the market is commercially attractive, how competitors operate, which regulations apply and what resources are required.

1. Understand the Japanese market before making an investment

The first step in entering Japan is understanding the market you intend to serve. A company should avoid assuming that a product that performs well in another country will automatically succeed in Japan. Different markets have different customer expectations, competitive environments, distribution channels, regulations and commercial practices.

1.1 Define your business objective

Start by identifying why your company wants to enter Japan. Possible objectives include:

  • Selling products to Japanese consumers
  • Providing software to Japanese businesses
  • Establishing partnerships with Japanese distributors
  • Working with Japanese manufacturers
  • Providing professional services to Japanese clients
  • Establishing a regional office or Japanese subsidiary
  • Using Japan as a base for broader regional operations

These objectives require different levels of investment and preparation. An international software company may initially want to test demand without establishing a large local operation. A manufacturing company may need local suppliers, regulatory checks and long-term operational planning. The entry strategy should reflect the actual business objective.

1.2 Identify the specific market segment

Japan should not be treated as one homogeneous customer group. Even within the same industry, customers may have different needs depending on organization size, location, budget and purchasing responsibilities.

Customer segment Possible needs
Small businesses Affordable tools and simple implementation
Medium-sized businesses Centralized monitoring and professional support
Large enterprises Advanced capability, integration and compliance
Technology companies Specialist solutions and developer tools
Professional service firms Protection of sensitive business information

1.3 Investigate market demand

A commercially useful market has customers who experience a problem your product can solve, understand the value of your proposed solution, have the ability and willingness to pay, can access your product, can receive the necessary support, and have a reason to choose your business over alternatives. The important question is not simply whether competitors exist — it is whether your business can offer something that a defined group of customers genuinely values.

1.4 Use reliable market research

  • JETRO — market entry, business establishment, regulations, taxes, human resources, visas and support services. jetro.go.jp/en/invest
  • Statistics Bureau of Japan — population, households, employment, prices and economic conditions. stat.go.jp/english
  • METI — industries, economic policy, trade and business development. meti.go.jp/english
  • Industry associations, competitor websites and professional networks

Use these sources together rather than relying on a single report. Market statistics may describe the overall economy, but they do not necessarily establish demand for your particular product.

2. Conduct practical market research

Once the target market is defined, the next step is to investigate whether the business opportunity is realistic. Market research should produce information that can influence actual business decisions.

2.1 Research your potential customers

For a B2B company, consider industry, organization size, location, number of employees, current providers, operational challenges, purchasing responsibilities, budget expectations and implementation requirements. For a consumer business, consider age range, purchasing habits, product preferences, price expectations, shopping channels, brand awareness and usage situations. These are research categories, not assumptions about every Japanese customer.

2.2 Interview potential customers

Customer interviews can reveal problems that are not obvious from online research. Useful questions might include: How do you currently manage this process? What problems do you experience? Which features are most important? What would make you consider changing providers? What information would you need before selecting a new system? Who participates in the purchasing decision? What support would you expect?

Avoid turning interviews into sales presentations. The purpose is to understand the customer, including reasons why a potential customer might not choose your product.

2.3 Analyze existing competitors

2.4 Study the customer journey

Stage 1: Problem recognition A company identifies an operational problem.
Stage 2: Research Employees investigate potential solutions through search engines, industry publications, recommendations and professional networks.
Stage 3: Comparison The company compares suppliers, features, pricing and implementation requirements.
Stage 4: Internal evaluation Relevant managers, technical staff and decision-makers evaluate the proposed solution.
Stage 5: Purchasing decision The company reviews commercial terms, implementation plans, support and contractual arrangements.
Stage 6: Implementation The customer begins using the product or service.

The journey varies by industry and company size. Research your own customers’ purchasing process rather than assuming one universal Japanese buying pattern.

2.5 Test demand before committing substantial resources

Possible approaches include interviews, online surveys, industry events, meetings with potential distributors, a localized landing page, product demonstrations, pilot projects and limited market-testing campaigns. A few enquiries do not automatically establish a profitable market — evaluate the cost of acquiring customers, expected revenue, sales cycle and resources needed to provide the service.

3. Decide how your business will enter Japan

There is no single entry method suitable for every international company. The right approach depends on the product, business model, investment capacity, operational requirements and long-term objectives.

3.1 Exporting products to Japan

Exporting may be an appropriate starting point for companies that manufacture or sell physical products. Potential advantages include testing demand before establishing a local company, using existing facilities and working with Japanese distributors. Companies must investigate applicable import requirements, product standards, customs procedures, taxes, labeling and distribution costs. Requirements differ significantly by product category.

3.2 Working with a Japanese distributor

A distributor may provide sales access, product promotion, distribution networks, customer communication, warehousing, logistics and local market knowledge. Before appointing a distributor, investigate its industry experience, existing customer relationships, sales capabilities, geographic coverage, product knowledge, financial capacity and conflicts with competing products. A distributor agreement should clearly explain responsibilities, pricing, territories, intellectual property, payment arrangements, customer ownership and termination.

3.3 Establish a partnership or joint venture

A partnership can provide access to local expertise, distribution, technical resources and business relationships. Carefully define ownership, financial contributions, management responsibilities, decision-making authority, intellectual property, profit distribution, confidentiality, exit arrangements and dispute resolution. Never assume that a verbal understanding is sufficient for a major long-term partnership.

3.4 Establish a Japanese subsidiary

A subsidiary is a separate legal entity established in Japan. Common structures include a Kabushiki Kaisha (株式会社 / KK) and a Godo Kaisha (合同会社 / GK). A subsidiary may be appropriate when the company wants to hire local employees, enter into local contracts, establish a permanent operational presence, build a local sales organization or operate independently as a Japanese legal entity. Obtain advice from qualified professionals familiar with Japanese company establishment.

3.5 Establish a Japanese branch

A branch is part of the foreign parent company rather than a separate Japanese subsidiary. It can be appropriate for businesses that want a more direct operational presence while remaining part of the overseas organization. A branch has different legal and administrative implications from a subsidiary.

3.6 Establish a representative office

A representative office can be useful during the research and preparation stage. It may allow market research, advertising and information gathering. However, a representative office generally cannot conduct ordinary revenue-generating business activities. JETRO explains the differences between representative offices, branches and subsidiaries in its official business setup guidance.

4. Understand company establishment and regulatory requirements

For some international businesses, entering Japan involves more than selling products remotely. They may need to establish a local legal entity, hire employees, open a business bank account or obtain specific licenses and permissions.

4.1 Research the legal requirements before incorporating

Investigate corporate structure, company name, business objectives, shareholder arrangements, directors and representatives, registered office requirements, capital arrangements, applicable permits, tax obligations and employment requirements. Certain business activities require licenses, permissions or notifications. Do not assume that registering a company automatically authorizes every type of commercial activity.

4.2 Understand the incorporation process

JETRO provides a detailed guide to establishing a business in Japan. A typical preparation process may include deciding intended business activities, selecting a legal structure, determining the company name, preparing incorporation documents, arranging capital contributions, completing registration, addressing tax and regulatory notifications, arranging banking and operational requirements, and obtaining any required licenses.

4.3 Business visas, taxation and employment

Company establishment and an individual’s permission to reside and work in Japan are separate matters. Consult official Immigration Services Agency information and qualified immigration professionals. Tax obligations depend on structure, activities and circumstances — potential considerations include corporate income tax, consumption tax, withholding tax, payroll-related obligations and cross-border transactions. Hiring employees involves employment contracts, compensation, working conditions, insurance, social insurance, payroll administration and applicable labor regulations.

Official resources: JETRO Setting Up Business · Immigration Services Agency

5. Localize your business for Japanese customers

Localization is not simply translating a website or changing a product label. It involves adapting the way a business communicates, sells, supports customers and delivers its product.

5.1 Localize the website

Review main navigation, product descriptions, pricing information, technical documentation, customer support information, FAQs, contact forms, privacy information and terms. A Japanese customer should be able to understand what the company offers, who it serves and how to obtain further information. Translation quality and customer relevance are more important than simply publishing a large number of translated pages.

5.2 Adapt product information & customer service

A software company may need to explain whether Japanese-language support is available, which local systems can be integrated, how data is handled, how implementation works and what technical support is provided. Decide how the business will respond to Japanese customers: support language, hours, local representatives, complaint handling and response times. Make accurate promises — do not advertise a Japanese customer service capability that the business cannot actually provide.

5.3 Review pricing, payment and branding

Depending on the product, businesses may need to consider Japanese yen pricing, applicable taxes, shipping costs, payment methods, subscription arrangements, invoicing, refund policies and distributor margins. Examine how the brand name, messages and product descriptions are understood in Japanese. A Japanese-language reviewer can identify confusing expressions before a major campaign launch.

6. Build relationships and find business partners

Business relationships can play an important role in international expansion. Finding a partner should be treated as a structured business process rather than simply collecting contact details.

6.1 Identify the right type of partner

Potential partners may include distributors, resellers, manufacturers, technology providers, system integrators, professional service firms, marketing agencies, logistics companies and industry associations. The right partner depends on the company’s objective.

6.2 Use professional networks, industry events and JETRO

Investigate industry exhibitions, trade fairs, business conferences, professional associations and technology communities. Before attending an event, identify the organizations you want to meet, prepare a clear business introduction and understand what type of collaboration you are seeking. JETRO offers resources for overseas companies interested in establishing or expanding operations in Japan.

6.3 Evaluate partners carefully

Investigate legal identity, business history, financial position, industry experience, existing customer relationships, references, operational capabilities, conflicts of interest and reputation. Agree on responsibilities in writing — payment terms, confidentiality, intellectual property, customer ownership, exclusivity, performance expectations and termination. Use appropriate legal review before signing.

7. Develop a marketing strategy for Japan

Once the business understands its target audience and operating model, it can develop a marketing plan. The objective should be to reach the right customers with useful information and a clear explanation of the product.

7.1 Build a Japanese-language website strategy

A Japanese website should help customers understand what the business offers, which customers it serves, what problems it solves, how the product works, why the company is credible and how to obtain further information. It should not simply reproduce the English homepage in Japanese.

7.2 Use Japanese SEO & consider paid search

A Japanese SEO strategy should include Japanese keyword research, localized content, technical SEO, search intent analysis, internal linking and performance monitoring. Research how Japanese customers describe their needs instead of relying entirely on direct English translations. For a detailed explanation, see SEO in Japan: The Complete Guide for International Businesses.

Paid search advertising can help reach customers who are actively searching. Investigate Japanese keyword selection, advertising costs, landing page quality, conversion tracking and expected customer acquisition costs. Start with clearly defined objectives and a controlled budget.

7.3 Choose social media platforms & develop educational content

Research which platforms are relevant to your particular customer segment. A focused presence with useful content is often more manageable than several inactive accounts. Educational content — industry guides, product comparisons, technical explanations, implementation tutorials, practical checklists and FAQs — can help build trust even when readers are not immediately ready to purchase.

8. Understand the cost of entering Japan

Market-entry costs vary considerably according to business structure, industry, operational requirements and growth objectives. There is no single cost figure that applies to every international business.

8.1 Separate initial and ongoing costs & prepare scenarios

Initial expenses may include market research, legal structure planning, incorporation, initial localization, website development and product adaptation. Ongoing expenses may include staff compensation, office expenses, accounting, marketing, software subscriptions, customer support, logistics and compliance. Develop conservative, expected and growth scenarios covering initial investment, monthly operating costs, expected revenue, gross margin, customer acquisition costs, cash requirements and break-even assumptions.

9. Common mistakes international businesses should avoid

  1. Entering Japan without validating demand — Interview customers, study competitors and test demand before making major commitments.
  2. Treating Japan as an extension of the home market — Develop a Japan-specific business and marketing plan.
  3. Choosing a business structure too early — Evaluate exporting, partnerships, branches, subsidiaries and representative offices before selecting a structure.
  4. Assuming translation is sufficient — Use professional localization and customer feedback.
  5. Selecting partners without proper checks — Conduct due diligence and use properly reviewed agreements.
  6. Underestimating operational costs — Prepare a complete operating budget and cash-flow forecast.
  7. Ignoring regulatory requirements — Identify applicable requirements before selling or commencing operations.
  8. Expecting immediate customer acquisition — Invest in useful information, relationships, product quality and consistent communication.
  9. Failing to provide local support — Define how Japanese-language enquiries, technical questions and complaints will be handled.
  10. Making decisions without measurable objectives — Establish measurable commercial milestones and review them regularly.

10. A practical 90-day Japan market-entry preparation plan

This is an example of how an international business could organize its initial research and preparation. It does not mean every company should establish a legal entity or begin selling within 90 days.

Days 1–30: Research and validation

  • Week 1: Identify the target customer, define the product, document the problem it solves, establish the intended business objective and initial assumptions.
  • Week 2: Review industry conditions, identify competitors, investigate pricing, research relevant regulations and identify potential distribution channels.
  • Week 3: Prepare interview questions, identify relevant businesses or consumers, conduct initial interviews, record recurring problems and unmet needs.
  • Week 4: Review customer feedback, compare competitors, estimate potential costs, identify major risks and decide whether further investment is justified.

Days 31–60: Develop the entry strategy

  • Week 5: Evaluate exporting, investigate potential distributors, consider partnerships, review subsidiary and branch options, obtain professional advice where needed.
  • Week 6: Review the English website, identify Japanese content requirements, translate and professionally review important materials, adapt product explanations, plan Japanese customer support.
  • Week 7: Identify potential partners, research industry associations, explore trade events, prepare a business introduction and arrange relevant meetings.
  • Week 8: Estimate initial investment, calculate operating costs, prepare revenue scenarios, evaluate cash requirements and identify major financial risks.

Days 61–90: Prepare for controlled market testing

  • Week 9: Publish Japanese-language information, prepare relevant landing pages, set up analytics and Search Console, review website functionality.
  • Week 10: Introduce the product to relevant prospects, arrange demonstrations, gather questions, review customer feedback and evaluate enquiry quality.
  • Week 11: Confirm distribution arrangements, review legal and compliance requirements, finalize customer support processes, check payment arrangements.
  • Week 12: Review customer interest, evaluate expected profitability, identify remaining risks, compare alternative entry models and decide whether to proceed, revise or delay.

12. Frequently asked questions about entering the Japanese market

1. Is Japan a good market for international businesses?

Japan may be attractive to businesses in a range of industries, but suitability depends on the product, customer demand, competition, operational requirements and financial resources. Investigate your specific opportunity rather than relying on general statements about Japan’s economy.

2. Can a foreign company do business in Japan without establishing a Japanese company?

Depending on the activity and circumstances, a foreign company may be able to serve Japanese customers from overseas or work through distributors and commercial partners. Regulatory, tax, contractual and operational considerations may apply. Obtain professional advice about your specific activities.

3. What is the difference between a Japanese subsidiary and a branch?

A subsidiary is a separate Japanese legal entity. A branch is part of the foreign parent company. They have different legal, administrative and tax implications. The appropriate structure depends on the intended business activities and long-term objectives.

4. What is a representative office in Japan?

A representative office is generally used for preparatory activities such as market research, advertising and information gathering. It generally cannot conduct ordinary revenue-generating business activities.

5. How much money is required to enter Japan?

There is no universal amount. Costs depend on the entry model, industry, legal structure, personnel, office requirements, localization, distribution and other operating expenses. Prepare a financial model based on your actual requirements.

6. Do I need a Japanese business partner?

Not every international company needs a Japanese business partner. However, a suitable partner may provide local knowledge, distribution access, implementation support or customer relationships. The decision should depend on the business model and the value the partner can genuinely provide.

7. Do I need a Japanese-language website?

For businesses targeting Japanese-speaking customers, a professionally localized Japanese website can be very valuable. It helps customers understand the product, research relevant information and communicate with the business. Maintain it accurately rather than treating it as a one-time translation project.

8. How can I find customers in Japan?

Potential channels include organic search, paid search advertising, industry events, professional networks, business associations, distributors, strategic partnerships, social media and direct business development. The appropriate channels depend on the intended audience and purchasing process. See also Customer Acquisition in Japan.

9. How long does it take to establish a company in Japan?

The timeframe depends on the corporate structure, documentation, participants, registration procedures and other requirements. JETRO’s establishment guidance explains the main stages. Consult relevant professionals for a realistic timetable based on your circumstances.

10. Do I need a visa to establish a business in Japan?

Company establishment and an individual’s permission to reside or work in Japan are separate matters. The appropriate residence status depends on the person’s circumstances and intended activities. Consult the Immigration Services Agency and a qualified immigration professional.

11. Can I test the Japanese market before opening an office?

Depending on the product and regulatory requirements, businesses can investigate demand through research, customer interviews, online content, distributors and other market-testing methods. This can help reduce the risk of committing substantial resources before validating the opportunity.

12. Is Japan suitable for SaaS companies?

Japan may offer opportunities for international software companies, but the opportunity depends on the product and intended customers. Investigate localization, integration, support, security requirements, pricing and the existing competitive environment.

13. Should I use a distributor or sell directly?

The decision depends on the company’s resources, product, customers and operational model. Distributors may provide local access and sales capabilities. Direct sales may give greater control over customer relationships. Evaluate both against their costs and benefits.

14. How important is customer support in Japan?

Customer support can influence customer confidence, implementation success and long-term relationships. Establish clear support responsibilities, communication channels and realistic service commitments.

15. Where can foreign companies get official help when entering Japan?

JETRO is an important starting point for foreign companies considering investment or business expansion in Japan. Its English-language resources explain business establishment, legal and regulatory matters, investment information and support services. jetro.go.jp/en/invest

Conclusion: Enter Japan with preparation, not assumptions

Japan can be an important market for international businesses, but entering it successfully requires more than launching a localized website or finding a distributor. Companies need to understand their customers, validate demand, evaluate competitors, choose an appropriate entry model and prepare for the operational requirements of doing business in Japan.

They should also consider localization, customer support, financial planning and the regulations that apply to their products and services. A careful market-entry strategy does not require every decision to be made immediately. In many cases, the most sensible first step is to conduct research, speak with potential customers and understand the commercial opportunity before committing substantial resources.

The strongest market-entry decisions begin with understanding the customer, validating the opportunity and preparing for the realities of the local business environment.
