The Marketing Fishbone Framework

Sustainable business growth and customer value are the outcome of nine interconnected systems. Weakness in any branch constrains the whole.

Why a fishbone view of marketing

Most marketing discussion focuses on the visible tip of the system: campaigns, media spend and conversion rates. The fishbone (Ishikawa) structure forces attention onto the upstream causes that determine whether those downstream activities can succeed.

The central outcome is deliberately broad: Sustainable Business Growth and Customer Value. Growth without value is fragile; value without growth is often invisible to markets. Both depend on the health of the branches below.

How to use this framework

For any brand or category under study, work systematically through each branch. Ask the diagnostic questions. Identify which data already exists and which is missing. Note where a single weak branch is currently limiting overall performance. The goal is diagnosis, not checklist completion.

A. Market

The external context in which the business competes. Market conditions set the ceiling and the tempo for every other branch.

What it covers

  • Market size and growth rate
  • Category maturity and lifecycle stage
  • Competitive structure (concentration, fragmentation)
  • Consumer and cultural trends
  • Seasonality and cyclical patterns
  • Economic conditions and purchasing power
  • Regulatory and policy environment

Why it matters

Even excellent products and brands struggle in declining or oversupplied categories. Conversely, a rising category can mask mediocre execution for a period. Market diagnosis prevents both false optimism and premature abandonment.

Diagnostic questions a marketer should ask ▼
  • Is the category growing, stable or contracting in the relevant geography?
  • What is the concentration of competitors (CR4 or Herfindahl index if available)?
  • Are new entrants appearing from adjacent categories or new business models?
  • Which cultural or technological shifts are expanding or shrinking demand?
  • How sensitive is demand to economic cycles or disposable income?
  • Are there regulatory changes that will alter cost structures or access?
Data that should be collected ▼

Industry association reports, government statistics (e.g. national accounts, retail sales indices), company filings of listed competitors, trade press, specialist research houses. Prefer multi-year series over single-year snapshots. Note geography carefully — global figures often hide large regional differences.

Business decisions it influences ▼

Market entry or exit, geographic expansion priorities, investment intensity, competitive response posture, category creation versus category participation strategies.

B. Consumer

Who buys, why they buy, what stops them, and how their expectations evolve.

What it covers

  • Demographics and life stage
  • Psychographics and lifestyle
  • Purchase motivations (functional and emotional)
  • Emotional and functional needs
  • Purchase barriers and friction
  • Customer expectations and standards
  • Loyalty, switching and repertoire behaviour

Why it matters

Products and brands succeed or fail in the minds and budgets of specific people. Aggregate market data can hide the fact that the brand is only relevant to a shrinking or low-value segment. Understanding barriers often reveals more actionable insight than understanding benefits alone.

Diagnostic questions ▼
  • Who is the primary purchaser versus the primary user or influencer?
  • What job is the customer hiring the product to do?
  • What emotional states or social signals are associated with purchase or use?
  • Where does friction appear in discovery, evaluation, purchase or use?
  • How high are switching costs, and what triggers repertoire expansion?
  • How do expectations differ by generation, culture or income band?
Data and methods ▼

Customer interviews, ethnographic observation, review mining, survey panels, loyalty and CRM data, social listening (with care about platform bias), purchase panel data where available. Combine quantitative reach with qualitative depth.

C. Brand

The set of associations, expectations and permissions that the business owns in the minds of customers and stakeholders.

What it covers

  • Brand positioning and frame of reference
  • Brand identity system (visual, verbal, behavioural)
  • Heritage and authenticity claims
  • Brand personality and tone
  • Brand promise and delivery consistency
  • Recognition, reputation and equity
  • Perceived value and differentiation

Why it matters

Brand is the primary mechanism for capturing a price premium and for reducing customer search and risk costs. Weak or confused brands force the business to compete on price, distribution muscle or short-term promotion — all of which compress margins over time.

Diagnostic questions ▼
  • What is the brand’s current positioning in the customer’s mind relative to key competitors?
  • Are identity codes (colour, form, language, ritual) distinctive and consistently applied?
  • Does the brand promise match actual product and service delivery?
  • What associations are strong, which are weak, and which are unwanted?
  • Is equity concentrated in a few products or distributed across the portfolio?

D. Product

The tangible and intangible offering that customers actually receive and experience.

What it covers

  • Innovation pipeline and novelty
  • Quality, materials and construction
  • Functionality and performance
  • Design and aesthetic codes
  • Packaging and unboxing
  • Assortment breadth and depth
  • Lifecycle management and renewal
  • After-sales support and serviceability

Why it matters

Brand can create desire, but product must deliver satisfaction and justify repeat purchase or recommendation. Chronic product weakness eventually erodes even strong brands. Conversely, superior product can sometimes overcome weaker brand and distribution for a period.

E. Commercial

How the business captures and protects economic value from the customer relationship.

What it covers

  • Pricing strategy and architecture
  • Gross and contribution margins
  • Revenue mix and growth quality
  • Promotional intensity and dependency
  • Product bundling and tiering
  • Channel economics and trade terms
  • Customer acquisition cost and lifetime value

Why it matters

Attractive top-line growth can mask deteriorating unit economics. Commercial diagnosis reveals whether the business is creating value or merely transferring it through discounts, channel subsidies or acquisition subsidies.

F. Communication

How the brand and product are made known, understood and culturally present.

What it covers

  • Advertising and media strategy
  • Public relations and earned coverage
  • Influencer and creator partnerships
  • Events, sponsorships and experiences
  • Content and brand storytelling
  • Partnerships and collaborations
  • Social presence and search visibility

Why it matters

Communication is necessary but never sufficient. Its effectiveness is constrained by the quality of the product, brand clarity and distribution. Measuring communication in isolation produces misleading conclusions about what is actually driving commercial results.

G. Customer Experience

Every touchpoint after the decision to engage, including service, environment and ongoing relationship.

What it covers

  • Physical and digital store environment
  • Customer service quality and recovery
  • Personalisation and recognition
  • Unboxing, delivery and first use
  • After-sales service and repair
  • Loyalty programmes and community
  • Relationship continuity across channels

Why it matters

Experience converts trial into preference and preference into advocacy. Poor experience turns marketing spend into a leaky bucket. Superior experience can justify premium pricing and reduce the need for constant acquisition pressure.

H. Distribution

How and where the product is made available, and what that availability signals about the brand.

What it covers

  • Flagship and controlled retail
  • Department and specialty retail
  • Mass and discounter channels
  • E-commerce and marketplaces
  • Direct-to-consumer models
  • Wholesale and international routes
  • Selective versus intensive distribution

Why it matters

Distribution simultaneously creates access and shapes brand perception. Over-distribution can erode exclusivity; under-distribution leaves demand unfulfilled. Channel conflict and grey markets are chronic risks for brands with strong demand and imperfect control.

I. Intelligence

The organisation’s ability to observe, interpret and act on signals from the market and its own operations.

What it covers

  • Sales and operational data
  • Customer reviews and feedback
  • Market and competitor research
  • Campaign and channel effectiveness
  • Customer lifetime value analysis
  • Product performance and returns
  • Emerging opportunity scanning

Why it matters

Without reliable intelligence, the other branches operate on assumption and lag. Strong intelligence systems convert experience into learning and allow earlier detection of both threats and opportunities.

How weakness in one branch affects the system

Consider a brand with strong product innovation and compelling communication, yet weak distribution control. The product may generate demand that is captured by grey-market sellers or unauthorised retailers, damaging price integrity and customer experience. Or consider excellent brand equity and distribution but chronic product quality issues: marketing spend then accelerates disappointment and erodes the equity it was meant to build.

The practical implication is that diagnosis should always ask: Which branch is currently the binding constraint? Resources applied to non-binding constraints produce little improvement in the overall outcome.

Research exercise

Select one brand you know well. Score each of the nine branches from 1 (severe weakness) to 10 (clear strength) based on publicly available evidence. Identify the two lowest scores. Write a one-page note explaining how those weaknesses are currently limiting sustainable growth or customer value, and what evidence would be needed to confirm or revise the diagnosis.
